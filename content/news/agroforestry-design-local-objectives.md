Two farms can receive the same planting recommendation and face very different decisions. One household may prioritise a reliable food supply, while another needs income during a particular season. One site may have the labour needed for a proposed management regime; another may not. A technically interesting combination of trees and crops is therefore only the beginning of a design conversation.

## Start from suitability, not from a universal winner

FAO's agroforestry module describes system selection as dependent on land conditions, household or community needs and available resources. It also recognises competition between components and recommends small-scale testing and cost–benefit analysis. The source does not support treating every agroforestry arrangement as equally beneficial in every setting.

FarmTree's interpretation is that design should begin with a short, explicit account of what the farmer is trying to achieve and what cannot be compromised. The working process below is a recommendation for structuring that discussion, not a claim that a particular system will deliver a guaranteed result.

## Make the objectives specific enough to compare

“Improve livelihoods” is too broad to distinguish between candidate designs. Ask what the household or enterprise needs to change: the timing of receipts, the availability of a product, exposure to a particular risk or the amount of work required at a difficult point in the season. Keep the answer in the farmer's terms before translating it into model inputs.

Record constraints alongside objectives. A proposal that performs well on an environmental indicator may still be unacceptable if it requires an unaffordable period without receipts or an unavailable labour input. Do not hide those constraints inside a weighted score that nobody outside the modelling team can interpret.

Where several people share responsibility for the farm, identify differences in priorities rather than assuming a single household preference. A useful design meeting can end with an unresolved trade-off, provided it is visible and there is a plan to address it. Apparent agreement produced by an ambiguous objective is less useful.

## Compare a small set of genuinely different options

Choose candidate designs that answer the stated problem in different ways. Avoid producing many nearly identical scenarios simply because the software can run them. The distinction should be understandable: a different management schedule, component arrangement, establishment sequence or product emphasis.

Describe what each option asks of the people managing it. Which operations are added? Which existing operations change? What information would the farmer need to decide whether the proposal is feasible? A scenario diagram can be a useful conversation aid, but it should not replace an explanation of the working calendar.

Include a credible comparison with the current system or another locally relevant option. The purpose is not to guarantee that the new design wins. It is to reveal what changes, what remains uncertain and which assumptions drive the difference. A result that favours retaining or adapting an existing practice can be a legitimate outcome of the analysis.

## Treat management as part of the design

A planting arrangement does not describe the full system over time. Record the management assumptions that make the scenario meaningful and identify who will implement them. If an assumed operation is unlikely to occur, the scenario should not quietly retain it because it produces a more attractive result.

Ask local practitioners to review the sequence. They may identify a conflict with another task, a practical access problem or a resource assumption that a remote analysis missed. Document the change made in response, including the reason. This keeps local knowledge connected to the technical work rather than presenting it as an informal consultation with no effect on the design.

For later review, distinguish a deliberate adaptation from an implementation gap. Both may alter the outcome, but they imply different learning. A farmer who changes a practice for a well-founded local reason should not disappear into a generic “non-compliance” category in a project-learning dataset.

## Decide what a small trial needs to teach

Before testing an option, identify the uncertainty the trial is intended to reduce. It may concern establishment, management feasibility, the suitability of an input assumption or the farmer's willingness to continue. Design observations around that question and state what would count as evidence against the current proposal.

Do not extrapolate a short observation period into a claim about the entire lifetime of a perennial system. Keep the observed period visible and explain which longer-term questions remain modelled or unknown. The related article on [Farm Valuation](/agroforestry-farm-valuation-timing/) discusses how horizon and timing assumptions affect financial interpretation.

Agree how findings will be reviewed with participants. A trial has limited decision value if the information is collected but the design remains fixed regardless of the result. Set a review point and identify which aspects of the proposed system can change.

## Use models to organise the discussion

The [FarmTree Platform](/platform/) provides a context for comparing scenario assumptions and linked indicators. A modelled comparison should help the team explain trade-offs; it should not displace the farmer's objectives or be presented as a universal recommendation. Preserve the assumptions behind each option so a later reviewer can understand why it was considered.

Finish the design phase with a concise decision record: chosen option, reasons, unresolved uncertainties, responsibilities and the observations that will trigger reconsideration. The strongest design is not necessarily the most elaborate one. It is the one whose intended role, practical demands and limits are understood by the people expected to manage it.
