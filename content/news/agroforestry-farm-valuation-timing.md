A favourable total over a long project horizon can conceal a difficult establishment period. That distinction matters to the farmer expected to finance and manage the transition. An agroforestry investment case should therefore explain the sequence of costs and receipts, not only the cumulative result at the end of the modelled period.

## Separate an attractive scenario from an affordable transition

FAO's agroforestry FAQs identify labour and resource costs, delayed returns and limited socioeconomic evidence as important considerations in adoption. FarmTree's approved Farm Valuation context brings production, costs, revenue and labour assumptions into a time-based scenario. Neither source establishes a guaranteed return for an individual farm.

The analysis below is a practical framework for reviewing those assumptions. It is not financial advice or a valuation of any named property. Its purpose is to make the economics of a proposed management system easier to discuss and challenge before an investment decision is taken.

## Build the calendar before interpreting the total

Start with the sequence of activities and the associated cash movements. Identify establishment expenditure, recurring operations and the assumed timing of saleable products. Keep quantities, prices and dates separately visible so the team can see which assumption changes when a scenario is revised.

A cost should not disappear merely because it is borne by another organisation. Distinguish the farmer's cash flow from the programme's cost and from any wider economic assessment. A subsidised input may reduce the farmer's immediate payment while still representing a real resource commitment in the project.

Likewise, distinguish a projected receipt from a secured one. Where an agreement or market assumption supports a price, record its scope and limitations. Avoid presenting an indicative future price as if it were a contract covering the entire horizon.

## Keep crop mix and management assumptions connected

A scenario should explain how the component activities fit together over time. If a proposed crop mix changes during establishment or as trees develop, the cost and revenue assumptions should reflect that sequence. An attractive receipt from one component should not be combined with an incompatible management assumption from another option.

Review the calendar with the people who understand the farm's operations. Ask whether the assumed work can be performed when the model expects it, whether inputs are available and whether the handling or sale of products requires additional capacity. These questions are often more useful than adding another decimal place to the output.

Treat household labour explicitly. Even when no wage is paid, the work competes with other uses of time. Record how labour is represented and, where relevant, compare the result under alternative assumptions. Do not switch between paid and unpaid labour conventions across scenarios without explaining the effect.

## Choose a horizon that answers the decision

The period used for comparison should be stated prominently. A short horizon may omit later events; a long horizon may depend heavily on assumptions that are difficult to verify today. Neither choice is automatically wrong, but both require explanation.

When options have different production cycles, identify how the comparison treats activities or assets remaining at the end of the period. Avoid giving one option an unexplained advantage through a generous terminal assumption. A reviewer should be able to distinguish value arising within the modelled period from value attributed beyond it.

If discounting is used, state the rate, its rationale and whether the comparison is consistent about inflation and currency. These are assumptions to review, not universal constants supplied by the concept of agroforestry. Show how a material change in the assumption affects the conclusion rather than reporting only one preferred result.

## Test the assumptions that can reverse the decision

Sensitivity analysis is most useful when it addresses a specific exposure. Which price, cost, timing or implementation assumption could make the proposed transition unaffordable or change the ranking of options? Begin with those questions instead of applying the same percentage adjustment to every input.

Consider combinations where they are relevant. A delayed receipt and a higher financing need may belong to the same operational story rather than two unrelated scenarios. Explain the scenario coherently, without presenting it as a probability forecast unless there is an appropriate basis for doing so.

Keep observed values separate from chosen planning assumptions. Where a number comes from a field record, retain the source and date. Where it is an estimate, identify who selected it and why. The model can be transparent even when some inputs remain uncertain; it becomes misleading when that uncertainty is concealed.

## Report the result from the farmer's perspective

Alongside any summary indicator, show when the farm faces a financing gap, what drives it and who is expected to cover it. A proposal that is attractive at programme level may still require a transition arrangement that has not been secured for participating farmers.

Use the [Farm Valuation Proof Story](/proof-stories/farm-value-projection/) as the product context for this time-based comparison, not as evidence of a particular investment's performance. The article on [context-specific agroforestry design](/agroforestry-design-local-objectives/) addresses how financial priorities fit alongside other farm objectives.

The useful endpoint is a reviewable scenario pack rather than a single promise: the activity calendar, source assumptions, cost and receipt sequence, horizon, sensitivities and unresolved financing questions. Take that pack back to farmers and local specialists before treating the preferred scenario as an implementation plan.
