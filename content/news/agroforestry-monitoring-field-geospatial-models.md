A monitoring programme can contain thousands of observations and still leave a decision-maker unsure what has actually changed. More layers do not automatically create stronger evidence. The useful starting point is a clear division of work: what is observed in the field, what is inferred from geospatial data and what is estimated by a model?

## Tools are part of a method, not a substitute for one

FAO describes SEPAL as a platform for accessing and analysing satellite data to support forest and land-use monitoring. Its 2026 tree-crop mapping guidance emphasises that no single geospatial dataset gives a complete account of a plot and that assessments need to reflect local landscape conditions.

Those sources support a complementary approach, not a claim that combining datasets automatically validates an agroforestry project. The operational framework below is an editorial recommendation for organising evidence and review. It is not a carbon-credit methodology, a verification opinion or a claim of formal recognition for FarmTree or another tool.

## Define the decision and the unit together

List the decisions the programme expects monitoring to support. A farmer considering a management adjustment, a programme team reviewing implementation and an external reviewer examining an outcome may need different information. Explain which decision each indicator serves before adding it to a dashboard.

Define the unit being described: an observation point, a plot, a farm, a project area or a wider landscape. Give each a stable identifier where appropriate and document how the units relate. Do not treat a result calculated for one scale as if it were independently established for every smaller unit inside it.

Set the reporting period with the same care. Identify whether an indicator describes a moment, a seasonal observation, an interval or a future scenario. A date in a filename is not enough if readers cannot tell whether it refers to collection, processing or publication.

## Preserve what was actually observed

For a field record, retain the method, date, relevant location information and quality status required by the programme. Make corrections traceable. If an observation is rejected, preserve the reason in the review record instead of silently removing inconvenient data.

Keep the collection procedure manageable for the people expected to perform it. Test instructions before scaling, and review whether different collectors interpret the same field consistently. Agree how uncertainty or an inaccessible location should be recorded rather than encouraging a guessed value to complete a required field.

The [smallholder data-governance article](/smallholder-data-governance-trust/) addresses participation and information sharing. Monitoring design should include those arrangements from the start, especially when evidence moves from a local collection team to several downstream organisations.

## Give geospatial evidence an explicit purpose

Record whether a geospatial layer is being used to define an area, locate possible change, guide sampling or support a particular classification. Keep its version, coverage and reference period available to reviewers. Where the intended use differs from the provider's stated purpose, explain how suitability was assessed.

Establish a process for disagreement between a mapped result and other evidence. Do not discard a field record solely because it differs from the map, or declare the map wrong solely because one observation differs. Examine the time, location, definitions and quality of both before recording a conclusion.

The article on [distinguishing tree-crop farms from forests](/mapping-tree-crops-farms-forests/) explores that classification problem in more detail. Its central operational implication is to preserve uncertainty long enough for it to be reviewed, rather than forcing every case into a convenient final category.

## Keep modelled indicators visibly separate

A modelled value should carry its scenario, input set and model version. Identify which inputs were observed and which were selected as assumptions. If an input is missing, record how the gap was handled and whether it limits the use of the result.

For scenario work in the [FarmTree Platform](/platform/), keep the link between the planning question and the selected indicators clear. A projected outcome is not the same as a monitored result. Updating the scenario with later observations may improve its relevance, but it does not turn every remaining estimate into a measurement.

When a model changes, compare results systematically before replacing a portfolio report. Choose representative cases, retain the earlier outputs and explain material differences. Readers should be able to distinguish a change in the model from a change in the management system or a newly observed condition.

## Build an evidence trail that another team can follow

Maintain a concise record linking each reported indicator to its source files, transformations, assumptions and review status. The objective is not to overwhelm readers with technical detail. It is to make the reported result reproducible by an appropriately qualified reviewer with authorised access.

Record exclusions and limitations alongside the result. A total should state what area and period it covers, which records were unavailable and how missing information was treated. Avoid presenting apparent completeness when part of the project is still waiting for observation or quality review.

Assign owners for collection, processing and acceptance. These responsibilities may sit with different organisations. Agree how changes are communicated and how the team will prevent an obsolete dataset from remaining in a downstream report after the source has been corrected.

## Scale the review process as well as the collection

Before expanding, test one complete reporting cycle from observation to decision. Ask a reviewer unfamiliar with the workflow to trace a small set of results back to their evidence. Use any ambiguity to improve the instructions and handover, not merely to repair those individual records.

A useful monitoring system ends with a decision and a documented basis for it. It may support implementation review, identify a field investigation or reveal that an outcome cannot yet be established. Treat that last result as information rather than failure to fill a dashboard: an explicit evidence gap is safer than an unsupported claim at scale.
