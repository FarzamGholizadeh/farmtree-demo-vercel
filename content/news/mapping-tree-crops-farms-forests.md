A shaded farm can look convincingly like forest in a satellite image. That resemblance is not a minor cartographic inconvenience when a map informs supplier screening or a land-use decision. Before accepting a coloured polygon as an answer, project teams need to understand what the map classifies, which observations support it and where the classification remains uncertain.

## What the 2026 guidance establishes

FAO's *Forest monitoring good practices: mapping tree crop commodities* addresses cocoa, coffee, rubber, palm oil and agroforestry systems. It is non-binding methodological guidance, not a certificate of regulatory compliance. Its central distinction is between the vegetation a sensor observes and the land use a decision-maker needs to establish.

The publication describes persistent difficulty observing crops beneath dense canopies, even with finer imagery. It calls for representative reference data, independent accuracy assessment and explicit reporting of confusion between commodity, forest and other land classes. Its convergence-of-evidence approach brings different datasets together rather than treating one map as a complete account of a plot.

The following review questions translate that technical context into an operational briefing. They are editorial recommendations for commissioning and using maps, not additional FAO requirements or a substitute for the applicable due-diligence process.

## Write down the decision before choosing the map

Specify whether the task is locating likely production areas, checking an individual supplier record, identifying places for field inspection or estimating an area total. These are different decisions. A map useful for directing survey teams may not justify an adverse finding about an individual farm.

For each proposed use, state the relevant geography and date. Ask the supplier to explain what its categories mean in ordinary language. Does a cocoa category describe a crop detected directly, an inferred production system or a parcel supplied through another source? That distinction should survive into the final briefing rather than disappear behind a simplified legend.

Also define the consequence of an error. If a false classification could prevent a producer from participating in a programme, the review process should not stop at a model score. Specify how contradictory evidence will be examined, who has authority to review it and what happens while the case is unresolved.

## Ask what lies outside the training examples

A procurement discussion often centres on the quantity of data. Ask about its composition as well. Which production systems, management stages and neighbouring land types are represented? Where did labels come from, and what checks were used when interpreters disagreed?

Request an account of gaps that is specific to the intended deployment area. A statement that the model works across a large region is not the same as evidence about the farms under review. The useful answer identifies the situations in which the provider has little reference evidence and explains how those situations will be handled.

Do not treat every uncertain area as a reason to collect everything again. Review whether relevant evidence already exists, whether permission allows its use and whether its date fits the question. Where another survey is necessary, plan it around the unresolved decision rather than repeating a generic data-collection exercise.

## Review disagreements instead of averaging them away

When two maps disagree, preserve the disagreement as a review item. Check definitions, dates, boundaries and methods before deciding that one is correct. A difference may arise because the maps answer different questions, not because one processing team made a simple mistake.

Keep an evidence note for each material case. It should identify the disputed area, the competing interpretations, the evidence examined and the reason for the conclusion. If the available evidence cannot resolve the case, retain an unresolved status. An empty certainty field is more honest than a confident label chosen only to complete a dashboard.

Separate corrections to a parcel record from changes to the mapping model. Both may be appropriate, but they have different implications. A locally verified correction does not establish that all similar-looking pixels elsewhere should receive the same label. Equally, a new model version should not silently replace the evidence used for an earlier decision.

## Make the supplier handover reviewable

Ask for a short, intelligible handover pack alongside the map. Include its purpose, geographical coverage, reference period, category definitions, version identifier, validation summary and known limitations. Record the files actually used, not merely the name of a web service whose output may change later.

Assign responsibility for updates. A team should know how it will learn that a dataset has been revised, whether earlier results need reconsideration and how corrections can reach downstream users. Keep a route for producers and local partners to submit evidence without requiring them to understand the underlying image-processing software.

The [smallholder data-governance discussion](/smallholder-data-governance-trust/) examines that participation problem. Data quality and fair treatment are linked operationally: a technically sophisticated system still needs a usable way to correct a record that affects someone's farm.

## Keep map evidence in its proper role

For agroforestry planning, a land-use map is one component of the evidence base, not a direct measurement of future production, carbon or income. The [FarmTree Platform](/platform/) provides a separate context for scenario comparison. Teams should record which mapped information informed a scenario and which assumptions still require field or specialist review.

A sound acceptance decision is therefore conditional and specific: this dataset, at this version and date, is suitable for this stated use, with these limitations and this review process. That is more useful than describing a map as universally accurate. It allows investment in mapping to support decisions while preserving a clear boundary between observation, interpretation and assurance.
