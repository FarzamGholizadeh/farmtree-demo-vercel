Restoration planning becomes difficult when a national ambition is treated as if it were already a farm-level specification. A broad target can establish direction, but it does not select the appropriate intervention for every parcel or explain how a project will demonstrate progress. Agroforestry teams need an intermediate layer: a measurable land-use plan that connects the local problem, the proposed action and the evidence used to judge it.

## Understand what the national-plan milestone represents

The European Commission's 1 September 2026 announcement describes submission of draft national restoration plans as an implementation milestone for the Nature Restoration Regulation, which entered into force in August 2024. The plans address delivery towards 2030 and provide a longer-term strategic view towards 2040 and 2050. The Commission links restoration to ecosystem condition and resilience, while distinguishing planning from the work needed to implement it.

For an individual programme, a national plan is a source of policy context to examine. It is not evidence that a particular agroforestry design has been approved, funded or shown to restore an ecosystem. The planning method below is FarmTree editorial interpretation, intended to help teams ask more precise questions before committing resources.

## Define the ecological problem before selecting the activity

Begin with a description of the condition the project intends to change. “Plant trees” describes an activity; it does not fully describe an ecological objective. A team should be able to explain the problem it has observed, the location and scale of that problem, and the reasons for considering a particular intervention.

Use the available local evidence to test the connection. If the concern is loss of habitat connectivity, the position and management of an intervention may matter differently from a project focused on exposed soil. Do not assume that the same design serves both objectives equally well. Record competing priorities and ask the appropriate specialists to review them.

The starting condition should be dated and spatially explicit. A photograph can help communicate a condition but rarely provides the entire baseline. Keep the underlying observations, their coverage and their limitations. If a project is still at a reconnaissance stage, say so rather than presenting preliminary information as a complete assessment.

## Distinguish delivery indicators from condition indicators

A practical project dashboard should not use one number to represent both implementation and ecological change. Trees planted, work completed or land under an agreement can describe delivery. They do not, alone, establish that the intended environmental condition has improved.

Write down the question each proposed indicator answers. Who needs the answer, at what interval and for which decision? This helps prevent the programme from collecting information that is easy to count while overlooking information needed to adjust management. It also makes the monitoring burden more visible before agreements are signed.

Choose a proportionate set of indicators with qualified ecological input. Avoid building an impressive-looking list that nobody has the capacity to maintain. Where an indicator changes slowly, explain how interim observations will be used without treating them as proof that the final objective has been reached.

## Match the spatial scale to the claim

A farm-level intervention and a landscape-level objective may be connected, but they are not interchangeable units of evidence. Explain whether a result concerns the treated plot, a participating group of farms or a wider landscape. Keep the area used in each calculation explicit.

For a hypothetical corridor project, a planted strip on one farm may be an important contribution while the wider connection remains incomplete. Describing the contribution accurately is more credible than describing the whole corridor as restored. The same logic applies when a programme has secured agreements for only part of its intended area.

Use maps to reveal those distinctions, not to hide them behind a single project boundary. Mark proposed, agreed, implemented and monitored areas separately where those states differ. Update the map when the project changes and preserve earlier versions used in decisions.

## Put maintenance and revision into the plan

Implementation does not end with establishment. Specify who will maintain the intervention, who can authorise a management change and how a problem reaches the person responsible for responding. A plan that identifies only the planting contractor may leave the longer-term responsibilities unclear.

Consider how the programme will respond if observations contradict the expected direction of change. Define a review process rather than a predetermined positive conclusion. A monitoring system is useful when it can identify the need to alter an intervention, not only when it confirms that scheduled activities occurred.

Financial planning should reflect those responsibilities. The [FarmTree Solutions](/solutions/) framework can help organise comparisons between management scenarios, but projected indicators are not observed restoration outcomes. Keep the scenario file linked to, and distinguishable from, the field-monitoring record.

## Use the policy context without promising a funding outcome

Check the current national plan and the relevant implementation arrangements before describing a project as aligned with a particular measure. Record the source and the date of that assessment. An emerging policy priority may justify further investigation without establishing an entitlement to public support.

For teams comparing implementation options, the related discussion of [CAP plans and measurable outcomes](/cap-agroforestry-performance-planning/) addresses the separate question of programme support and performance evidence. Neither article substitutes for national eligibility rules or the responsible authority's decision.

The practical takeaway is to prepare an objective-to-evidence sheet before presenting the project as restoration: starting condition, intended change, intervention, indicator, scale, responsibility and review point. If those elements cannot be connected coherently, refine the plan before adding a stronger claim to the proposal.
