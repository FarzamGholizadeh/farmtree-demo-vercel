A farm boundary can move quickly from a field collector's device into a buyer's screening system. The farmer may have much less visibility over that journey. For project teams, the governance question begins before collection: who needs the record, what decision will it support and what practical control will the producer retain when it is shared?

## The question behind FAO's 2026 publication

FAO announced new monitoring guidance on 3 June 2026, including work on inclusive smallholder data governance. Preferred by Nature, which prepared the governance report for FAO, describes its scope as the collection, governance and sharing of production-plot data in commodity supply chains. Its cases span cocoa in Colombia, coffee in Kenya, rubber in Viet Nam, palm oil in Indonesia and soy in Brazil.

That published scope is the starting point here. The recommendations below are an original operational framework, not a reproduction of the report's detailed findings. They do not claim that every country has the same data-rights framework or that a single consent form can satisfy all applicable obligations.

## Give every field a reason to exist

Begin with a data inventory that an operational manager and a producer representative can both understand. For every requested field, record the purpose, intended recipient and decision it supports. Separate what a specific process requires from what would simply be convenient to have.

For example, a team should ask whether a contact detail must travel with a plot geometry or can remain with the organisation arranging field visits. The answer depends on the workflow and applicable rules. The discipline is to make that decision deliberately rather than export a complete farmer profile whenever a boundary is needed.

Review optional information separately. If it has no defined use, there is little reason to make its collection a condition of participation. If a future use is proposed later, evaluate it at that point instead of assuming that the first collection permanently authorised every subsequent activity.

## Explain the journey in terms people can use

A technical explanation of a database is not enough. Describe who will receive the information, what they intend to do with it and how their decisions could affect participation in the programme. Use language and communication channels appropriate to the people involved, with a way to ask questions after the collector has left.

Avoid promising anonymity merely because a name has been removed. Ask a qualified reviewer to assess what combinations of fields could identify a person or expose a sensitive location. Document that review alongside the sharing arrangements rather than treating de-identification as an automatic property of a file format.

Where consent is the proposed basis for an activity, test whether the choice is meaningful in practice and obtain appropriate legal advice. Do not assume that a signature proves understanding or that refusing an optional use should automatically prevent access to an unrelated service. Equally, do not invent a universal right or obligation where the governing framework has not been established.

## Distinguish collection, stewardship and legal rights

The organisation collecting a record, the organisation maintaining it and the person whose farm it describes may be different. Allocate operational duties explicitly while separately establishing the applicable legal rights. A data-stewardship agreement should not be presented as resolving land tenure or ownership of a farm.

Name the party responsible for keeping the record accurate, answering requests and communicating corrections. Define how a producer can reach that party without needing an account on an unfamiliar platform. Programme staff also need a clear escalation route when two recipients hold conflicting versions of the same record.

Agree how information can be transferred if a service provider changes. Portability is not merely the ability to download a spreadsheet: the receiving team needs definitions, identifiers, permissions and enough history to understand the record. Review the cost and practical steps before the relationship ends.

## Build correction into the normal workflow

A disputed boundary should have an identifiable case owner and status. Record what is disputed, which evidence is available and what decision is awaiting review. Keep a distinction between a collector's error, an outdated observation and an unresolved disagreement about the underlying facts.

Where a corrected record has already been shared, define how recipients will be notified. Otherwise, an accurate source can coexist with an outdated screening result downstream. Track acknowledgement where the consequence warrants it, and preserve enough history to explain why a decision changed.

The linked article on [mapping farms and forests](/mapping-tree-crops-farms-forests/) addresses uncertainty in geospatial interpretation. A producer-facing correction process complements technical validation; it should not be used as an excuse to skip validation before deploying a map.

## Budget for participation, not only collection

Include time for explanation, local-language support, review and correction in the operating budget. Assign responsibility for these activities beyond the initial survey. A programme can collect records successfully yet fail to maintain a relationship through which those records remain understandable and useful.

Ask producer organisations which steps impose repeated effort and which information they can realistically maintain. Test the proposed workflow with a small group before scaling it. Report what was learned and changed, without describing consultation as proof that every participant agrees with the final design.

For organisations using [FarmTree's Solutions](/solutions/) in a wider planning process, these governance decisions sit alongside the modelling task. A scenario may draw on farm information, but a useful calculation does not itself settle permissions, participation or the responsibilities of downstream recipients.

The practical deliverable is a working arrangement: a justified field list, named stewards, explained uses, controlled sharing, a correction route and resources to maintain it. Review it with producer representatives and relevant specialists before expanding collection. Trust should be supported by observable practices, not left as a claim in a project description.
